\title{\Large \bf Short Paper: Gnomon: A Streaming Storage Engine for Temporal Graphs}

% Double-blind: no author identification.
\author{Anonymous submission}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Temporal graphs whose edges are immutable timestamped events---audit
streams, agent execution traces, interaction logs---are ingested as
never-ending, near-sorted streams; queried along time and causality;
and retained under time-based policies. No deployed storage layer
serves this combination: entity-keyed graph stores demote the
timestamp to a payload attribute, columnar and LSM engines pay a join
per traversal hop, versioned temporal graph stores assume few
property-rich versions per entity, and statically time-sorted layouts
answer queries well while being unable to ingest at all.

\sys keys storage on time itself: each timestamp maps to a virtual
address by a constant-time shift, and demand paging absorbs temporal
sparsity, so no index is built or maintained. Ingestion becomes a
lock-free $O(1)$ append independent of arrival order; retention
becomes a page release over an address prefix; compaction is explicit
and off the ingest path; queries remain correct \emph{during}
concurrent ingestion through a per-slot publication protocol; and
because engine state is a pure function of the edge multiset, recovery
is re-ingestion. We evaluate \sys against a columnar engine, three CSR
layouts, and the most recent versioned temporal graph store, with
element-wise answer verification, on fourteen datasets including a
billion-edge enterprise corpus, and show that keying storage on time
wins the streaming and traversal axes by orders of magnitude while
conceding bounded, measured costs on the structural axis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A growing class of graphs is built from \emph{events}. A system call
touches a file; an agent invokes a tool; a host authenticates to
another; a user replies to a message. Each edge carries the moment it
happened, never changes afterwards, and arrives as part of a stream
that does not end. For a storage system this combination is unusual
and demanding: writes are append-only and unbounded, reads are
constrained by time and causality rather than by key, and the
retention policy---keep the last $N$ days---is itself a predicate on
the write key. A single endpoint under ordinary load emits tens of
thousands of such events per second, and a fleet multiplies that, so
what the layout does with the time dimension determines the write
path, the read path, the eviction path, and the recovery path at once.

Existing storage engines all approach this workload with a layout
designed for something else. Dynamic graph
stores~\cite{wheatman2021ppcsr, shen2026dcsr, dhulipala2019aspen,
fuchs2023sortledton, pandey2021terrace, deleo2021teseo, zhu2020livegraph,
kumar2019graphone} key storage on source-vertex identity and treat the
timestamp as payload. A time-constrained query then becomes a
structural scan with a per-edge filter, and a retention pass has to
find expired edges wherever vertex order scattered them. Columnar and
LSM engines~\cite{raasveldt2019duckdb} have the opposite problem:
ordered by time, they answer range filters well, but every hop of a
traversal is a fresh join against the edge table. Versioned temporal
graph databases~\cite{li2026tva, hou2024aeong} solve a different
problem---retrieving the valid \emph{version} of an updated
entity---and their structures assume few, property-rich versions per
entity, which an event stream violates by construction. A statically
time-sorted CSR answers temporal queries close to optimally, but it is
not a storage engine: its only update path is a full rebuild, and
during that rebuild it is blind.

\sys keys storage on time. The starting observation is that a 64-bit
virtual address space is orders of magnitude larger than the time
range of any recorded workload, so instead of maintaining an index
from timestamp to location, we let the timestamp \emph{be} the
location:
\begin{equation}
A(T) \;=\; A_{\mathrm{base}} + \big((T - T_{\mathrm{start}}) \gg S\big)\times P,
\label{eq:mapping}
\end{equation}
one subtraction and one shift, with the operating system's demand
paging absorbing temporal sparsity at no data-structure cost.

What follows from that layout is the point of the paper. An edge's
home is computed, not searched, so ingestion is a wait-free slot
claim; the computation ignores arrival order, so late events cost
nothing extra; retention is a monotone predicate on time, so expiry
releases an address prefix; and nothing is updated in place, so
recovery is replaying an append-only log through the normal insert
path. None of these is engineered on top of the layout; each is a
consequence of it.

The idea is not that time-ordering is new---log-structured file
systems~\cite{rosenblum1991lfs} and LSM trees~\cite{oneil1996lsm} have
organised writes by arrival for three decades, and time-series
stores~\cite{pelkonen2015gorilla} exploit temporal locality directly.
What those designs share is that time orders the data but an auxiliary
structure still maps keys to it: an LSM keeps sorted runs and a
manifest, a time-series store keeps per-series chunk indexes. \sys
removes that level. Because the key \emph{is} the time and the time
\emph{is} the address, there is no run, no manifest, and no chunk
index to maintain, merge, or recover---which is why compaction here is
optional rather than structural, and why recovery is replay rather
than log analysis~\cite{mohan1992aries}.

The design also has costs, and a storage paper owes their measurement
as much as their mitigation. A layout that refuses to organize by
entity must pay something on entity-keyed operations. A layout that
never sorts during ingestion must either scan unsorted neighborhoods
or sort them later. We measure both, bound the second with an explicit
compaction contract, and report the one operation where a competing
design beats us outright (\cref{sec:eval}).

We contribute: (i)~a storage design that keys temporal graphs on time,
with proofs for its addressing, space, granularity, and concurrency
properties (\cref{sec:design}); (ii)~a publication protocol under
which readers never observe a torn or unpublished edge during
lock-free multi-writer ingestion (\cref{thm:conc}); and (iii)~an
evaluation across fourteen corpora and five competing layouts that
verifies element-wise answer equality before reporting any latency,
separates storage cost from algorithm cost, and measures what a static
structure silently loses while it rebuilds (\cref{sec:eval}).

\subsection{Time-keyed addressing}
\Cref{eq:mapping} maps a timestamp to a slot in one anonymous memory
mapping (\cref{fig:arch}). Two properties turn that arithmetic into a
storage layout.

\begin{theorem}[Addressing]
\label{thm:addr}
For any timestamp $t$, the slot holding every edge stamped $t$ is
computed in $O(1)$ worst case, with no data-dependent memory access.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The map is one subtraction and one shift. Two timestamps land in the
same slot exactly when $\lfloor (t-T_0)/2^S \rfloor = \lfloor
(t'-T_0)/2^S \rfloor$, i.e.\ when they share a window, so every edge
stamped $t$ is registered under that one index and no other slot can
hold one. No search structure is consulted, so the bound is
unconditional rather than expected-case. A B-tree or LSM index gives
$O(\log E)$ with pointer chases; a hash index gives \emph{expected}
$O(1)$ with worst-case collisions.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Space]
\label{thm:space}
Let $P$ be the page size and $W_{occ}$ the number of non-empty
windows. Resident timeline memory is at most $W_{occ}\cdot P$ plus
page-table overhead the kernel materializes lazily; untouched windows
cost zero bytes, whatever the virtual reservation.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The timeline is an anonymous mapping of pointer slots. A page becomes
resident only when first written, and a slot is written only when its
window receives its first edge. Resident pages are therefore at most
the number of pages holding at least one occupied slot, which is at
most $W_{occ}$ since each occupied slot dirties one page. Untouched
ranges are never faulted in and never appear in the page table.
\end{proof}

Sparsity is absorbed by the MMU, not by a structure the engine
maintains. The mapping is \emph{anonymous}, so the failure modes
catalogued against mmap in databases~\cite{crotty2022mmap}---torn file
pages, SIGBUS on truncation, eviction stalls---do not apply: we use
mmap as an allocator reserves address space, not as a buffer pool.

The shift $S$ sets how much time one slot covers. Too coarse and
windows overfill; too fine and the timeline sprawls. \sys picks $S$
from stream statistics, and the choice is bounded.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\small
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{event count $E$, span $S_{\mathrm{span}}$, target occupancy $\tau$}
\KwOut{shift $S$}
$D \leftarrow \max(1, \lfloor E/\tau \rfloor)$ \tcp*{windows we want}
$w \leftarrow \max(1, \lfloor S_{\mathrm{span}}/D \rfloor)$ \tcp*{width we want}
$S \leftarrow \lceil \log_2 w \rceil$ \tcp*{width we can address}
\Return{$S$}
\caption{Adaptive granularity selection. Runs once on a stream
prefix; re-running on a later prefix changes only future windows,
since \cref{eq:mapping} is evaluated per edge at insert time.}
\label{alg:shift}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Granularity]
\label{thm:shift}
For $E \ge 2\tau$ events with target occupancy $\tau$, the selected
shift yields mean window occupancy $\mu \in [\tau/2,\, 4\tau)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The selector computes $D=\lfloor E/\tau \rfloor$ desired windows, a
desired width $w=\lfloor S_{\mathrm{span}}/D \rfloor$, and takes the
smallest $S$ with $2^S \ge w$, so $2^S \in [w, 2w)$. The realized
window count $W=\lceil S_{\mathrm{span}}/2^S \rceil$ then satisfies
$D/2 < W \le D+1$, giving $\mu = E/W \ge E/(D+1) \ge \tau/2$ and
$\mu < 2E/D < 4\tau$.
\end{proof}

The two factors of two are real, not slack in the analysis: one comes
from rounding the window width up to a power of two, which shift
addressing cannot avoid, and one from the ceiling on the window count.
Across our corpora a single target yields per-window densities from 32
to 32{,}768 edges, and the bound holds at both ends.

\subsection{Lock-free ingestion, correct concurrent reads}
\label{sec:design-ingest}
An insert computes its slot (\cref{thm:addr}), claims an index in the
window's block with one \texttt{fetch\_add}, and installs overflow
blocks by CAS. Every failed CAS means some other insert succeeded, so
ingestion is lock-free and the slot claim itself is wait-free. Nothing
sorts and nothing rebalances, so arrival order does not matter---which
is what separates \sys from every sorted-on-ingest design as soon as
events arrive late or out of order.

Readers are protected by a publication protocol instead of locks. A
writer fills an edge's payload first and stores its timestamp
\emph{last} with release semantics. Fresh arena pages are zero-filled,
so a zero timestamp marks a slot that is not yet published; readers
acquire-load the timestamp and skip those. Vertex-index references
publish through their block pointer the same way. One extra ordering
rule makes the fast read path safe as well: an append clears the
bucket's \emph{sorted} flag \emph{before} it bumps the bucket counter.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\small
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetKwProg{Fn}{}{}{}
\Fn{\textsc{Append}$(e)$}{
  $b \leftarrow$ block for slot $(e.t - T_0) \gg S$ \tcp*{\cref{thm:addr}}
  $b.\mathit{sorted} \leftarrow \bot$ \hfill \textsf{(release)}\;
  $i \leftarrow \textsc{FetchAdd}(b.\mathit{count}, 1)$ \hfill \textsf{(acq-rel)}\;
  \lIf{$i \ge b.\mathit{cap}$}{install overflow block by CAS; retry}
  write $e.\mathit{src}, e.\mathit{dst}, e.w$ into $b[i]$\;
  $b[i].t \leftarrow e.t$ \hfill \textsf{(release: publishes the slot)}\;
}
\Fn{\textsc{Expand}$(v, t_{lo}, t_{hi})$}{
  \ForEach{bucket $b$ of $v$}{
    $n \leftarrow b.\mathit{count}$ \hfill \textsf{(acquire)}\;
    \lIf{$b.\mathit{sorted}$ \textsf{(acquire)}}{binary-search $b[0..n)$}
    \lElse{scan $b[0..n)$, skipping $t = 0$}
  }
}
\caption{Publication protocol. The two release stores and the order of
lines 3--4 are what \cref{thm:conc} rests on: clearing \emph{sorted}
before bumping \emph{count} is what lets a reader trust a binary
search, and storing $t$ last is what makes a half-written slot
invisible.}
\label{alg:publish}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Concurrent reads]
\label{thm:conc}
Under this protocol no query observes a torn, partial, or unpublished
edge during concurrent multi-writer ingestion, and a reader that finds
the sorted flag set may binary-search the counted prefix.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the first claim: a reader only accepts a slot whose timestamp is
non-zero, and the writer's release store of the timestamp happens
after its payload writes, so an acquiring reader that sees the
timestamp also sees the payload. A slot is written once and never
mutated, so no torn state exists after publication.

For the second: a reader loads the counter (acquire) and then the
flag. Suppose it observes the flag still set. Any append that could
have extended the counted prefix cleared the flag before bumping the
counter, and the reader's acquire on the counter synchronizes with
every bump it observes; so if any such bump were visible, the
corresponding clear would be visible too and the flag would be unset.
The observed prefix is therefore exactly the one compaction verified
and published, and binary search over it is sound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The compaction contract}
Appends leave a vertex's neighborhood in arrival order, so a
time-windowed expansion has to scan it: $O(d)$. An explicit
\texttt{compact()} pass sorts each neighborhood by timestamp once, and
afterwards expansion binary-searches in $O(\log d + k)$ for $k$
results. Compaction is deliberately \emph{not} on the insert path. We
measured what happens when it is: an early build that compacted after
every batch ingested at 11K events/s, because each batch re-walked the
whole index, against 3.3M events/s once the pass was moved off the
path---the same $O(\text{index})$-per-batch cost a sorted structure
pays by design.

Queries are correct in both states; compaction changes their cost, not
their answers. This is the paper's central trade. A statically sorted
structure gets its query speed by making sorting \emph{mandatory}, and
pays for it with a rebuild window during which its answers are wrong
(\cref{sec:eval-stream}). \sys makes sorting \emph{optional} and pays
a bounded constant until it runs. The contract is: \emph{always
appendable, always correct including mid-ingest; fast expansion after
a compaction you schedule when convenient.}

\subsection{Retention and durability}
Retention is a monotone predicate on time, so under
\cref{eq:mapping} it is an operation on an address prefix.
\texttt{evict\_before($t$)} walks the timeline prefix, merges the block
extents it finds, and returns whole pages to the OS with
\texttt{madvise}; readers of other windows are untouched. There is no
scan of the edge set and no tombstone compaction, because expiry and
address order are the same order.

Durability uses a property most databases do not have: engine state is
a pure function of the edge multiset. Edges are immutable, never
updated, and deletion is retention policy rather than semantics. The
log is therefore just framed, checksummed batches of raw edges; an
asynchronous writer overlaps it with insertion; and recovery is
replaying the log through the ordinary insert path. There is no undo
phase, no redo phase, and no idempotence bookkeeping, because
replaying a batch twice is indistinguishable from the multiset having
contained it twice---and it cannot, since each batch is framed once. A
torn tail frame fails its length or checksum test and is discarded.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
\sys is $\sim$6,300 lines of dependency-free C++17: the engine, nine
graph algorithms including bidirectional time-respecting traversal,
converters for two audit-log formats, and seven correctness suites
(among them a multi-writer/multi-reader stress test of
\cref{thm:conc} and an end-to-end synthetic-provenance fixture).
Interior windows of a range scan are copied without any timestamp
comparison---only boundary windows can contain out-of-range
edges---and boundary scans are AVX2-vectorized over the packed
edges. Source, converters, proofs, and every experiment driver will
be released.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\textbf{Setup.} 20-core Xeon, 188\,GB RAM, Ubuntu 20.04. Fourteen
corpora spanning five orders of magnitude in size
(\cref{tab:breadth}): LANL enterprise
authentication~\cite{lanl2015data} (1.05B events, 17,666 hosts, 58
days), DARPA TC whole-system provenance (CADETS 22.5M and THEIA 97.3M
information-flow edges), and 12 public temporal graphs from 60K to
113M edges. Baselines: DuckDB v1.5.5 through its C API in the same
process; a time-sorted CSR (\texttt{tcsr}, the strongest static
competitor); an arrival-order entity CSR (\texttt{ucsr}, what an
ingesting entity-keyed store holds between rebuilds); a topology-only
CSR; and TVA~\cite{li2026tva} via its released artifact. Before any
latency is reported, we check that the engines returned the same
answer element by element. Latencies here are heavy-tailed, so we
report medians with tails or results binned by answer size, never
means.

\subsection{Ingestion}
\label{sec:eval-ingest}
Across the 13 corpora of \cref{tab:breadth}, \sys ingests at
1.9--9.0M~events/s on eight threads, and the one optional compaction
pass costs at most 6.0\,s on the largest of them (113M edges). Single-threaded, matched against
the competing engine's own thread count, the rates are 11.9--15.5M
events/s. On the billion-edge LANL corpus \sys sustains 6.8M events/s
under 12 concurrent writers \emph{while} four readers issue validated
queries: 48,636 queries, 780M delivered edges checked, zero protocol
violations, and a final edge count that matches exactly. With the
write-ahead log on, durable ingestion reaches 4.2M events/s under
64\,MB group commit against 7.4M volatile---the limit there is the
disk's measured 156\,MB/s sequential bandwidth, not the engine---and
1.2M with an fsync per batch.

Driving TVA's artifact with the same single-threaded event stream did
not complete wiki-talk's 7.8M events within a 90-minute cutoff, at
41.6\,GB RSS (\cref{fig:streaming}a). The cause is structural rather
than incidental: in an event stream every repeated $(src,dst)$ contact
extends one edge's version chain, which is exactly the structure its
hopscotch layout must keep within bounded offsets, so its design
assumption---few, property-rich versions per entity---inverts. A
statically sorted CSR is absent from this comparison for a simpler
reason: it has no ingest path to measure.

\subsection{Time-respecting traversal}
\label{sec:eval-traversal}
On multi-hop time-respecting traversal run to fixpoint, \sys leads
DuckDB by $150$--$700\times$ across all answer sizes; at $|R|>1{,}000$
the median is 12\,ms against 2.43\,s (\cref{fig:temporal}a). The
reason is structural: each hop costs a relational engine a join
against the edge table. The time-sorted CSR stays $2.7\times$ faster
than \sys at high degree, which is the measured price of refusing to
sort during ingestion; \cref{sec:eval-stream} prices what the CSR pays
for that privilege.

Against the \emph{ingesting} entity-keyed layout the ordering flips
with graph density (\cref{fig:temporal}b). We normalise to cost per
delivered edge, because the corpora span 60K to 1.05B edges and
absolute times are not comparable across them. An arrival-order
adjacency has to scan a whole neighborhood to find the edges inside
the query window, so its per-edge cost rises from 26\,ns at degree 6
to 1{,}500\,ns at degree 59{,}517. \sys's compacted buckets amortise a
binary search over the same neighborhood, so its per-edge cost
\emph{falls} from 54\,ns to 15\,ns. The crossover sits near degree
100, inside the range event-stream workloads occupy.

Two more results bound the picture. On time-\emph{range} filtering,
DuckDB's home ground, \sys's vectorized scan wins the median
$10.6\times$ (77\,$\mu$s against 819\,$\mu$s over five seeds) with p95
at parity (981 against 1{,}105\,$\mu$s, overlapping ranges). And on
structure-only algorithms we decompose each run into the share spent
delivering adjacency, under executions verified identical by checksum.
Over 29 measurements on 11 corpora, \sys delivers at
$0.9$--$5.8\times$ the topology-only CSR optimum, median
$2.1\times$, and the spread separates into two regimes
(\cref{fig:structural}). Frontier-driven traversal touches only the
vertices it reaches and stays at $1.4$--$2.2\times$ regardless of
corpus size, including $1.4\times$ on the billion-edge corpus.
Whole-graph iteration such as PageRank or label propagation walks
every vertex's bucket chain on every round, where a CSR walks one
contiguous array, and that gap widens with size: $5.8\times$ at 69M
edges. This is the concession, it is the price of not organising by
entity, and it buys an ingest path the optimum does not have.

\sys's weakest operation is the full-graph as-of snapshot, answered by
enumerating and reducing: 218\,s on CADETS, whose hottest vertices
carry millions of events. That is what TVA's version-skipping is built
for, and we report it as its win.

\subsection{Rebuild versus stream}
\label{sec:eval-stream}
The time-sorted CSR's only update path is a full rebuild: 28.4\,s on
LANL. A query about the live window served from the pre-rebuild
snapshot is not stale but silently \emph{wrong}. On LANL, 68 of 100
such queries returned incomplete answers, losing 33.2\% of the true
result on average and 99.5\% in the worst case; an independent
wiki-talk measurement gives 34.1\% and 96.7\%
(\cref{fig:streaming}b). \sys ingests the same arrivals incrementally
at 3.3M events/s and has no blind window at all.

\sys's own optional compaction costs more than the CSR's rebuild on
LANL (76\,s against 28\,s), because it resolves a timestamp per
reference by chasing a pointer into the arena; resolving in arena
order instead would close most of that, and is future work. But the
comparison is between two different kinds of cost. The CSR's rebuild
is on the correctness path---without it the answers are wrong.
\sys's compaction is on the performance path---without it the answers
are right and expansion is slower. That difference is the whole point
of the contract.

\subsection{Retention, scale, and a real workload}
\texttt{evict\_before} releases 90\% of a 16.5M-edge history in 18\,ms
and returns 331\,MB of resident memory, $5$--$29\times$ faster than a
columnar \texttt{DELETE}. Both reclaim memory fully, so the win here
is speed alone---we had expected the columnar engine to mark rows
without reclaiming, and it does not. Across the corpora of \cref{tab:breadth} resident memory is
63--158\,B per edge against a 20\,B packed edge; the rest is the
vertex index and block headers. LANL loads in 255\,s at 96\,GB RSS with the reverse
index enabled.

As an end-to-end check on real ground truth: tracing all 749 labelled
red-team alerts of the LANL corpus to causal fixpoint takes 46.5\,s in
total (median 24.6\,$\mu$s, p95 353\,ms). On DARPA CADETS, a forward
trace from the documented exploit source reconstructs the published
attack chain---implant drop, privilege escalation, C2, injection
target---in causal order, inside an impact set that is 0.0093\% of the
graph, at 133\,$\mu$s.

\subsection{Negative results, kept}
Caching timestamps in vertex-index refs ($+50\%$ index memory for
$15\%$ traversal) was built, measured, and reverted; binary-search
expansion buys $2.5\times$ at degree 337 and nothing below degree 30;
and this comparison's original hypothesis---that the columnar engine
would mark deleted rows without reclaiming---was false.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Versioned temporal graph stores.} TVA~\cite{li2026tva} and
AeonG~\cite{hou2024aeong} organize versions of \emph{updated
entities} for snapshot retrieval; delta encoding and version-chain
locality, their central difficulties, do not arise in an append-only
event stream, and time-respecting reachability is not expressible
over their snapshot query surface. The two lines are complementary,
and measurably so in both directions
(\cref{sec:eval-ingest,sec:eval-traversal}): neither subsumes the other; they store
different things.
\textbf{Structural dynamic graph stores}~\cite{wheatman2021ppcsr,
shen2026dcsr, dhulipala2019aspen, fuchs2023sortledton} optimize the
entity axis and treat time as payload; the storage-read
decomposition bounds what \sys concedes on their axis, and none
answers time-respecting queries natively.
\textbf{Columnar/LSM engines}~\cite{raasveldt2019duckdb} own
predictable range-filter tails and lose traversal by orders of
magnitude. \textbf{mmap in databases.} The case against
mmap~\cite{crotty2022mmap} targets file-backed buffer management;
\sys's anonymous reservation sidesteps each cited failure mode.
\textbf{Provenance systems}~\cite{king2003backtracker,
pasquier2017camflow, zhang2025tapas, hassan2020omegalog} motivate the
workload's rates, and their reduction
techniques~\cite{lee2013loggc} exist because no storage layer made
retention affordable; serving them is an application of this engine,
developed separately. Corpora are from
SNAP~\cite{leskovec2014snap}, LANL~\cite{lanl2015data}, and DARPA
Transparent Computing.

\section{Conclusion}
Keying storage on time collapses the machinery an event stream
otherwise needs: the index becomes a shift, ingestion an
order-indifferent lock-free append, retention a page release, and
recovery a replay. The concessions are bounded constants.
